Bias Mitigation Notes for Models 1, 2, and 3
GE-79 Cognitive-Status Classification Project

Purpose
- This file explains how we tried to reduce or avoid the specific bias risks identified for the GE-79 dataset and our Models 1, 2, and 3.
- These steps reduce bias where possible, but they do not remove all bias from the dataset.
- The models should be treated as educational research models, not clinical diagnostic tools.


1. Geographic Bias

Targeted bias:
- GE-79 is a single-center study run in Boston.
- A model trained on this dataset may learn patterns specific to that hospital system, region, or recruitment pool.
- The results may not generalize to other regions, healthcare systems, or countries.

Steps we took:
- We clearly label the dataset as GE-79 and do not claim that the model generalizes outside this cohort.
- Models 1 and 3 are evaluated only within the GE-79 dataset using stratified cross-validation.
- We report results as within-dataset performance, not as external validation.
- Model 2 is a placeholder and is documented as not yet contributing final performance claims.

Remaining limitation:
- We cannot fully remove geographic bias without testing on external data from other hospitals, regions, or countries.


2. Age Bias

Targeted bias:
- The dataset focuses on older adults.
- The model may not apply to younger adults with Type 2 Diabetes.

Steps we took:
- We describe the project scope as older adults with Type 2 Diabetes.
- We avoid claiming that the model applies to younger Type 2 diabetic populations.
- The README and output descriptions frame results around the GE-79 cohort rather than all diabetes patients.

Remaining limitation:
- The model should not be generalized to younger patients unless it is retrained or externally validated on younger cohorts.


3. Selection Bias

Targeted bias:
- Participants were recruited into a research study.
- Research participants may be healthier, more motivated, or more medically engaged than the general patient population.

Steps we took:
- We report the cohort size and target distribution so readers can see the sample context.
- We avoid presenting the model as a general screening tool for all elderly diabetic patients.
- We use the same locked feature set across Models 1 and 3 so performance differences come from model behavior, not from changing who or what is included.
- Model 2 is kept as a placeholder until it can follow the same contract.

Remaining limitation:
- Selection bias cannot be fully corrected from inside the model because the dataset itself may not represent the broader patient population.


4. Survivorship Bias

Targeted bias:
- In GE-79 follow-up, fewer diabetes subjects have matching later-visit records than controls.
- If sicker diabetes patients dropped out or died before follow-up, complete follow-up data may overrepresent healthier survivors.

Steps we took:
- We used one visit per patient for modeling rather than conditioning the model on complete long-term follow-up.
- This avoids training Models 1 and 3 only on patients who survived or stayed enrolled through later visits.
- The scripts explicitly note one row per patient to reduce repeated-measures leakage and avoid overrepresenting patients with more complete records.

Remaining limitation:
- Survivorship bias may still exist in the available baseline/research cohort.
- The model cannot recover information about patients who were never enrolled or who were missing before the modeled visit.


5. Label Bias

Targeted bias:
- The target label comes from cognitive testing.
- Cognitive assessments can misclassify Normal vs Mild Impairment.
- The model learns from imperfect labels.

Steps we took:
- We use a clear binary target encoding: 0 = No Impairment and 1 = Impaired.
- We exclude MMSE and cognitive-status code fields from the model features to prevent target leakage.
- We report confusion matrices, macro-F1, and impaired recall instead of hiding errors behind one accuracy number.
- We describe the outputs as classification results, not as medical diagnoses.

Remaining limitation:
- If the original cognitive label is wrong, the model can still learn from that error.
- Reducing label bias further would require clinical review, repeated assessments, or alternative cognitive measures.


6. Education Bias

Targeted bias:
- Education strongly affects cognitive test scores.
- A model might predict education level rather than true cognitive impairment.

Steps we took:
- Education is not part of the locked final feature list used by Models 1 and 3.
- The selected predictors are biomarker and physiology-related variables, such as glucose, fasting glucose, blood pressure, vasoreactivity, perfusion, white-matter markers, and related clinical measures.
- We avoid interpreting model predictions as proof of true cognitive status independent of social or educational context.

Remaining limitation:
- Education can still affect the cognitive label used as the target.
- Even if education is not used as a feature, label bias from education-adjusted or non-adjusted cognitive testing may remain.


7. Racial Representation Bias

Targeted bias:
- GE-79 is overwhelmingly White, with very small counts for Black/African American, Latino, Asian, and Other/Unknown groups.
- Sparse subgroup counts prevent reliable race-level inference.

Steps we took:
- Race is not included in the locked final feature list used by Models 1 and 3.
- We do not make subgroup performance claims by race because the subgroup counts are too small.
- We avoid claiming fairness across racial groups based on this dataset.
- The model outputs emphasize cohort-level limitations and the need for external validation.

Remaining limitation:
- Removing race as a predictor does not remove racial representation bias from the dataset.
- Biomarkers, healthcare access, diagnosis timing, and recruitment patterns may still reflect structural inequities.
- Fairness cannot be established without a larger and more racially representative dataset.


8. Class-Imbalance Bias

Targeted bias:
- The dataset has more No Impairment patients than Impaired patients.
- A model can appear accurate by mostly predicting the majority class.

Steps we took:
- Models 1 and 3 use class_weight="balanced" to reduce majority-class dominance.
- We use 5-fold stratified cross-validation so each fold preserves the class ratio as much as possible.
- We report macro-F1 and impaired recall, not only accuracy.
- The visualizations explicitly compare each model against the majority-class baseline.
- Model 1 catches more impaired patients, while Model 3 has higher accuracy but lower impaired recall; both tradeoffs are shown.

Remaining limitation:
- The dataset still has only 20 impaired patients, so minority-class estimates are unstable.
- More impaired cases would be needed for stronger conclusions.


Model-Specific Notes

Model 1: Logistic Regression
- Bias-reduction role: baseline, transparent, and easier to interpret.
- Uses balanced class weighting.
- Catches 10 of 20 impaired patients in the current run.
- Has more false positives, but that tradeoff is visible in the confusion matrix.
- Useful for showing why accuracy alone is not enough.

Model 2: Decision Tree Placeholder
- Model 2 is intentionally documented as a placeholder/student guide right now.
- It does not currently produce final claims.
- When completed, it should follow the same bias controls:
  - same locked features from Model 0,
  - same target encoding,
  - same leakage controls,
  - same 5-fold stratified cross-validation,
  - same class_weight="balanced" setting,
  - same reporting of macro-F1 and impaired recall.
- Keeping it as a placeholder avoids presenting unfinished results as validated model performance.

Model 3: Random Forest
- Bias-reduction role: ensemble comparison model with feature-importance output.
- Uses balanced class weighting.
- Reports feature importance so users can inspect which variables influenced the model.
- Shows high accuracy but lower impaired recall, making the majority-class tradeoff visible.
- Does not replace the need for subgroup testing or external validation.


Bottom Line
- We reduced preventable modeling bias by controlling leakage, using one row per patient, locking the same feature set across models, using stratified cross-validation, applying balanced class weights, reporting minority-class recall, and documenting the limits of the dataset.
- We did not eliminate dataset-level bias from geography, age, selection, survivorship, education effects, label quality, or racial representation.
- The correct interpretation is: these models compare approaches inside GE-79, but they require external validation before use beyond this cohort.

VerifyWise Fairness Assessment

What was tested:
- VerifyWise was used to screen the cleaned GE-79 dataset across race, study group, diabetes status, hypertension status, and insulin use using cognitive_status_label as the target.
- VerifyWise reported low bias for the tested attributes, with statistical parity difference 0.000, disparate impact ratio 1.000, and demographic parity ratio 1.000.

How to interpret this:
- This means VerifyWise did not detect measurable parity differences under the selected fairness metrics.
- This does not prove that the dataset is unbiased.
- This does not prove that Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, or Random Forest predictions are fair.
- The VerifyWise run evaluated a ground-truth clinical label rather than model-generated predictions or deployment decisions.

Remaining limitations:
- Geographic bias, selection bias, survivorship bias, small sample size, limited racial representation, label uncertainty, and lack of external validation remain present.
- Model fairness should be assessed separately using subgroup analysis of model predictions if subgroup counts are sufficient.
- VerifyWise is one component of the broader Responsible AI review, alongside dataset auditing, bias review, SHAP interpretability, PROBAST-AI review, TRIPOD-AI reporting, and governance practices informed by the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and OECD AI Principles.
